Making 200 Million Messages Boring

An SAP upgrade meant pausing marketing journeys without interrupting critical customer messages. On another release, a journey import failed and customers missed a message. Both put the delivery plan to a real test.

The cutover and journey recovery come from client work at Vigorate. The audience example uses fictional numbers. Program metrics cover the wider work.

Two decisions from production

What could we pause during an SAP upgrade?

The cutover plan had to cover when Marketing Cloud journeys would pause and restart. Critical transactional messages still had to go out. I worked with the client’s IT and marketing teams and our developers to coordinate the timing and safeguards for more than 20 marketing journeys. The distinction between marketing and transactional messages changed what could be paused.

How do we reach customers a journey missed?

An import error left customers out of a live journey. I worked through recovery options with the client and developers and secured approval for a one-time send to the missed audience. In parallel, I coordinated the plan for a replacement data extension and SQL routing for future entries. That gave the client a way to address the immediate gap while the team worked on the recurring failure.

On a separate billing automation incident, I tracked restoration and confirmed that all missed bounce-report files had been reprocessed. The confirmation was for the reports that had been missed, so the client could close out the incident against a defined result.

Which customer gets resubscribed?

A resubscription change between SAP and Salesforce Marketing Cloud raised a question I brought to client IT and our developers. If a person changes their email address, what identifies the account whose consent should change? The matching rule we agreed to validate used customer ID alone. I led the client escalation and helped document six test scenarios, monitoring checkpoints and rollback considerations.

Here’s a fictional test that shows what the rule needs to prove. One customer has a new email address. Another customer still has the old address on file. A resubscribe request for the first customer should update that customer’s consent and leave the second record as it was. I’d ask QA to verify both records in Marketing Cloud before recommending release.

Which audience is ready?

Suppose an approved audience has 48,000 eligible records and the replacement segment has 47,400. Comparing the lists shows 900 people missing and 300 new additions. A gap of 600 records hides two different problems. I’d keep the approved file for this send if it still passes the current consent and exclusion checks, and ask the data team to explain both groups before switching sources.

I use the same rule when the thing that changes is creative. In one bilingual automotive email release, an alt-text correction meant the revised asset needed a fresh test and approval. I coordinated the revised tests and timing with the client and distribution partners.

How I run the work across teams

A late audience change can make an earlier test irrelevant. An urgent production issue can also take up the developer and QA time planned for the next release. I update the workback plan with the team and show the client what would move if we took on the extra work.

My role at each step

Brief and plan
I clarify the client’s requirements and work with Solutions Consulting as they define the scope, estimate and budget. I turn the agreed scope into a delivery plan and confirm the acceptance criteria with engineering and QA.
Build and test
I work through dependencies with engineering and data and review test results with QA. We agree which defects would block release before I take a recommendation to the client.
Release
I bring the recommendation and customer impact to the client’s release owner for approval. I agree with operations on what to watch and when to escalate.

A small copy revision may only need a named reviewer and a record of which version changed. A new audience source needs reconciliation, a consent check and test records for people who should stay out of the send. If it changes the agreed scope, I work with Solutions Consulting on the revised estimate and bring it back to the client.

What I ask before we go live

  • Can we reconcile the audience counts and explain the exclusions?
  • Does the approved version match the one QA tested?
  • Did we test someone who should never enter the journey?
  • Who can pause the release, and who checks the first results?

If a critical check fails, I put the recommendation and customer impact in front of the decision owner. A smaller release can work when the affected component can be isolated and the remaining work is ready.

Marketing needs to know who might get the wrong message. Engineering needs the exact source field and failing test. The sponsor needs the decision, the options and when they’ll hear from us again.

After launch, I review the first results with operations. If the counts or customer impact differ from what we expected, I follow up with the teams that can trace the issue and agree with the client on what to fix next.
