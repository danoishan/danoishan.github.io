Case 02 · lifecycle / operating model

Making 200 Million Messages Boring

The interesting part of high-volume lifecycle marketing is not the send count. It is building enough delivery control that a large, cross-functional communication program stops feeling heroic.

At scale, the campaign is not the system.

Enterprise lifecycle programs contain dozens of moving parts that are individually ordinary: audience logic, data extracts, content, approvals, SQL, journeys, QA, reporting and production releases. The risk comes from the interfaces between them.

When volume and concurrency grow, delivery can become dependent on individual memory: who knows which audience rule changed, which data source is late, which stakeholder still owes approval, or whether the final version is actually the version QA tested.

My goal was not to add project-management ceremony. It was to make the interfaces between teams visible enough that launches became boring.

The failure modes were predictable.

Most recurring problems could be grouped into a small number of patterns:

I treated delivery as an operating system.

Rather than manage each campaign as an isolated mini-project, I used a repeatable lifecycle that could flex by complexity. The important change was that every stage produced something the next stage could rely on.

Intake→Requirements→Data readiness→Build
Build→QA / UAT→Go / No-Go→Deploy→Monitor
RAID · budget · capacity · dependencies · change control

The cross-cutting controls mattered more than the boxes. A risk discovered during requirements needed to survive into launch planning. A capacity decision needed to be visible to both the client and delivery team. A late change needed an explicit impact rather than silently becoming “the new plan.”

The controls I cared about.

Requirements that can be tested

I prefer requirements that describe observable behaviour rather than vague intent. “Target lapsed members” is not enough. What qualifies as lapsed? Which source owns status? What happens to recently reactivated customers? Which exclusions override inclusion?

Decision logs

A useful decision log is not meeting minutes. It records the choice, alternatives, rationale, owner, date and conditions that would cause a revisit.

RAID as an active tool

Risks and dependencies were connected to real delivery decisions. A data dependency with no owner is not a “yellow status”; it is a blocker waiting for a date.

Commercial visibility

Retainer and project delivery require the same technical discipline as launch planning. Scope, burn, forecast and resource allocation are part of the system because unplanned work eventually becomes a quality or schedule problem.

What I deliberately did not standardize.

Process gets dangerous when it starts optimizing for itself. I did not want a 30-minute campaign to inherit the same ceremony as a complex data-driven journey.

Standardized

  • Ownership
  • Acceptance criteria
  • Release candidate naming
  • Risk / dependency visibility
  • Core QA evidence
  • Go/no-go criteria

Kept flexible

  • Creative iteration
  • Marketing strategy
  • Exact meeting cadence
  • Artifact depth by complexity
  • Technical solution when multiple valid paths existed

The operating model focused on interfaces between teams because that was where the highest delivery risk lived. It did not try to turn every specialist into a project manager.

Go/no-go should be an evidence review, not a discovery session.

Before a production release, the team should already know the answer to the questions below. The meeting exists to confirm evidence and make a decision, not to invent the readiness model live.

Requirements accepted
Audience counts reconciled
Suppression / consent validated
Content version frozen
Journey / automation logic tested
Negative test cases passed
Monitoring owner named
Rollback / stop path understood

At this scale, success is not “nothing ever goes wrong.” Success is that the team can see risk early, make an explicit trade-off, and respond without losing control of the broader program.

What this taught me.

The largest improvement in complex delivery often comes from better interfaces, not better individual performance. A brilliant developer cannot compensate for an ambiguous source-of-truth decision. A perfect campaign brief cannot compensate for an unresolved data dependency. A detailed schedule cannot compensate for hidden capacity.

The job is to make the system around good people reliable enough that quality does not depend on heroics.
