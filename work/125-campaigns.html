Decision snapshot.

The short version for someone skimming the case.

Executive viewOutcome → decision → proof
Business objectiveRun a national event and campaign program repeatedly across distinct markets without losing audience relevance or execution control.
What changed the answerFixed event dates, different local audiences and partners, an eight-person team and repeated handoffs across campaigns and live operations.
RecommendationTreat each market as part of one portfolio: segment locally, work backward from immovable dates and reuse a common operating rhythm.
Trade-offKeep the process repeatable without forcing identical messaging or market execution everywhere.

The program was national. The execution could not feel improvised.

Jobs Canada Fair operated across multiple provinces with a mix of employer outreach, attendee marketing, email campaigns, partner communication and in-person event delivery. The volume itself was manageable. The complexity came from repeating the work across markets without flattening everything into the same message or losing control of timing and ownership.

The real job was not “send more email.” It was create an operating rhythm that could repeat nationally while still feeling relevant locally.

Every market created another version of the same coordination problem.

Campaigns had different audiences, event dates, partners and operational dependencies. A missed handoff in one city could turn into a last-minute scramble; multiplied across a national calendar, that becomes the operating model.

I was leading an eight-person team, so the solution had to make priorities and ownership clear enough that the work did not depend on one person remembering every moving piece.

I treated each event as part of a portfolio, not a standalone project.

The repeatable structure was simple: know the audience, know the date that cannot move, know the dependencies and know who owns the next action.

STEP 1

Segment the audience

Match employer, candidate and partner communication to the market and event rather than defaulting to one generic national message.

STEP 2

Build the workback

Work backwards from fixed event dates so campaign, partner and operational dependencies surfaced early.

STEP 3

Run the rhythm

Use repeatable campaign and team routines so the next market could inherit what worked instead of starting from zero.

The team worked better when every handoff had a clear output.

I was not personally doing every creative, partner or operational task. My role was to keep the program moving by making sure the next person received the right context, timing and definition of done.

Across the program, I owned strategy and execution for more than 125 targeted email campaigns. The work was not sophisticated because of a single platform feature. It was sophisticated because of the volume, repetition and need to keep audience relevance, people and operations aligned across markets.

The metrics mattered because they reflected repeatability.

The program contributed to a 10% increase in customer retention and a 15% increase in new customer acquisition. Those are the headline numbers, but the more durable outcome was learning how to build a system that could support growth repeatedly across markets.

Each market became input to the next rollout.

Scale did not mean copying the exact same campaign six times. We kept the operating structure stable while using performance, partner feedback and on-the-ground execution lessons to adjust audiences, timing, messaging and coordination for the next market.

01

Launch

Run the market using a common operating rhythm and clear ownership.

02

Review

Capture what worked, where handoffs slowed down and which audience or partner assumptions changed.

03

Optimize

Adjust targeting, communication timing and operating steps based on real execution feedback.

04

Repeat at scale

Carry the improved pattern into the next city / province without rebuilding the process from scratch.

What carried forward into my technical delivery career.

The systems are different now, but the operating principles are almost identical. Today the dependencies may be identity, consent, source data, APIs, UX, engineering or release environments instead of venue logistics and local campaign calendars.

The transferable skill is recognizing that scale changes the problem. Once work becomes concurrent, cross-functional and repeated, success depends less on individual heroics and more on a clear operating model, durable handoffs and a feedback loop after launch.

What I would formalize earlier now

Today I would make the feedback loop more explicit from the start: define success measures before launch, name the owner for post-launch learning and decide which parts of the operating model are standardized versus intentionally local.

This role was where I first learned to manage the portfolio, the handoffs and the next iteration, not just the task in front of me.
