The challenge wasn't creating one more asset.

For a device or trade-in campaign, the creative had to match the offer, audience, channel and approved messaging. An English email, a French variant and an MMS execution could share the same objective without sharing identical copy, layouts or production requirements.

That made the client brief a delivery problem as much as a creative one: what was changing, which variants were affected, who needed to act and what had already been reviewed?

Keep the variations visible.

The campaign work involved coordinating creative inputs across several dimensions rather than treating every revision as an isolated request.

My job was to keep people and decisions connected.

As a client-facing delivery lead, I translated requests and feedback into actionable next steps for creative, development, data and QA teams. I coordinated production timelines, followed up on revisions, kept changing priorities visible and worked toward client sign-off.

I did not personally design every asset, build every message or own the underlying audience logic. My accountability was keeping the brief, reviewers, specialists, dependencies and release decisions moving together.

Feedback has to travel with context.

A comment like “update the device image” is incomplete if several language, offer or channel versions exist. The useful handoff identifies the affected asset, the requested change, the approval needed and whether a previous review must be repeated.

01

Clarify

Confirm the requested change and affected audience, channel, language or offer.

02

Route

Get creative and technical questions to the right internal owners.

03

Review

Coordinate revised materials, client feedback and any downstream testing implications.

04

Approve

Confirm which release version is ready and what remains open before launch.

The four stages above summarize the coordination approach; they are not a claim that every historical campaign used this exact documented process.

A polished asset still needs to launch correctly.

Creative approval and production readiness are different checkpoints. Beyond copy and visual review, the release needed appropriate checks for channel rendering, language, offer details, links, audience targeting and final client approval, with specialist QA supporting the technical validation.

When creative changed late, the question was whether that change affected only a visual element or invalidated something already tested or approved. Making that impact explicit reduced the chance of teams working from different versions.

What I can credibly claim.

I coordinated recurring bilingual email and MMS campaign production across client, creative, development, data and QA stakeholders, including offer and device variations, review cycles and launch handoffs.

This public case does not claim a measured conversion lift, reduced production time or personally produced creative assets. The evidence here is the scope of coordination and the operating decisions, not a fabricated performance number.

What carries into other creative formats.

Whether the output is an email, a motion asset or a web experience, the producer's central challenge is consistent: convert a changing brief into a sequence of owned decisions, protect the approved version and make sure the right people review the right thing before launch.

This case demonstrates multichannel campaign production. It does not, by itself, establish hands-on video, motion or web-design execution; those responsibilities should be supported by separate project examples.
